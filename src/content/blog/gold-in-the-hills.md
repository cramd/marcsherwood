*Why AI adoption needs a divining rod before it needs a roadmap.*

What if the first question an organization asked about AI had absolutely nothing to do with AI?

What if, instead of looking for places to deploy the technology, we went looking for the places where the business was quietly bleeding time, money and opportunity?

Every organization has workflows that people have learned to tolerate. Processes held together by spreadsheets, tribal knowledge and the occasional act of heroism.

These aren't necessarily failures. They're often the accumulated compromises of a growing business.

And somewhere among them, there's gold.

## The Divining Rod

![A divining rod points toward gold hidden among tangled business workflows.](/images/gold-in-the-hills/divining-rod.webp)

Imagine sending a prospector into the hills with instructions to dig a hole exactly six feet deep, in a predetermined location, by the end of the quarter.

They might do an excellent job of digging that hole. They might even finish ahead of schedule. Whether there's any gold at the bottom of it is an entirely different question.

This is the risk of starting an AI initiative with a technology decision or a fixed list of applications. We've committed to digging before we've established where the gold is.

I'd rather spend time with the people doing the work. Not just the people responsible for the work.

Watch someone prepare for a customer meeting. Follow a support ticket through its various incarnations. Find out what happens when a perfectly ordinary request falls outside the perfectly ordinary process.

Listen for a few phrases in particular:

- "I have to do this every Monday."
- "Only Susan knows how that works."
- "It's quicker if I just do it myself."
- "That's just the way we've always done it."

None of these are proof of a problem worth solving. They're signals worth investigating.

An interesting side effect of working with the same systems day after day is that we stop seeing some of their peculiarities. A workaround becomes a process. A process becomes a requirement. Eventually, someone new joins the company and is trained to perform the workaround.

The very people who understand a workflow best may no longer notice its most avoidable parts.

## From Prospector to Forensic Investigator

![A geological investigator traces a gold vein through rock samples and a field notebook.](/images/gold-in-the-hills/investigation.webp)

A divining rod is a metaphor for curiosity, not a measurement instrument. A hunch gets us to a promising location. Evidence tells us whether to dig.

Suppose a team says it spends too much time producing weekly reports. Is generating the report really the problem? Perhaps the data arrives late, two systems disagree, or the report itself is rarely read. A beautifully engineered AI report generator won't fix any of those underlying problems. It might simply make it easier to manufacture reports nobody needs.

Before building anything, I'd want to trace the workflow from beginning to end. How often does it happen? How much time does it actually consume? What causes rework? What does a good outcome look like, and who gets to decide?

Where possible, quantify those answers. Where we can't, acknowledge what we don't know.

The interesting thing about a good investigation is that the initial suspect isn't always the culprit.

## Working the Mine

Once we've identified several worthwhile opportunities, we have another problem: we can't mine all of them at once.

The temptation is to tackle the most visible or technologically interesting project. I'm far more interested in attacking the opportunities in descending order of expected return on investment, subject to feasibility and acceptable risk.

Consider a hypothetical workflow. Twenty people each spend three hours a week performing a repetitive task. If a new process reliably cuts that time in half, that's 30 hours a week of reclaimed capacity.

At an illustrative loaded labour cost of $75 an hour, the capacity is worth $2,250 a week before we account for building, operating, maintaining and reviewing the solution. That's not $2,250 in cash savings. The organization still needs to put those hours to good use.

Now compare that with an impressive AI project that saves a single executive an hour a month but requires a substantial investment to deploy and maintain.

Which would you dig for first?

Time isn't the only thing worth recovering. Reducing a costly error, accelerating a decision or removing a source of customer frustration might be worth far more. The important part is agreeing on the value and how we'll measure it before committing to the work.

Confidence matters too. A modest improvement with strong evidence and a short path to delivery can be a smarter investment than a spectacular theoretical return with very little evidence behind it.

And here's something that might sound odd in an article about AI adoption: sometimes the right solution is a SQL query, a better integration, a change in process or simply stopping something that shouldn't be done in the first place.

I'd happily take the gold without the AI.

## The Assay

![Brass scales compare real gold with fool's gold.](/images/gold-in-the-hills/assay.webp)

Digging up something shiny doesn't make it gold.

Launching a tool isn't the same thing as improving a business. A thousand API calls, an enthusiastic demo and a growing number of registered users tell us something, but they don't necessarily tell us whether we have created value.

We need an assay: a way of comparing the result against the original problem.

Establish the baseline. Test the solution with real examples. Instrument the things that matter: quality, cost, latency, exceptions, actual usage and the amount of human review required.

If a process used to take 30 minutes and now produces an answer in 30 seconds, that's encouraging. If someone then spends 40 minutes checking and correcting the answer, we have learned something rather different.

This is where observability belongs in the AI conversation. Not as a post-launch addition, but as part of the design. We need to understand what the system is doing, where it is failing and whether the people depending on it are actually better off.

Security, privacy and human accountability belong there too. Some tasks need human judgement no matter how impressive a model's output appears. A potential return doesn't excuse unacceptable risk.

## What Should We Have After 90 Days?

I'd argue that a good first 90 days of applied AI work should produce more than a strategy document.

We should have a ranked, evidence-backed list of opportunities. We should have taken at least one meaningful problem from discovery through a real pilot, and, where the evidence and risk allow, into everyday use. We should understand what improved, what didn't, and what it cost us.

If we haven't made a meaningful difference by then, that's another problem worth investigating. Did we misunderstand the work? Overestimate the return? Underestimate the difficulty of changing the process? Or were we unable to get the access, evidence or cooperation needed to deliver?

There may be perfectly good reasons. But we should know what they are.

And the roadmap? It should now be considerably more interesting than the one we could have written on day one.

## The Next Vein

Something wonderful happens when we involve the people whose work we're trying to improve.

A successful change can prompt someone to show us another piece of their job. Someone in a neighbouring team realizes they have a similar problem. We discover that two seemingly unrelated pain points share a common cause.

The first discovery informs the next one. What we learn changes the order in which we work the mine.

That's the kind of AI strategy I'd like to see more organizations adopt: not a hunt for excuses to use a new technology, but a continuous investigation into how the business can work better.

The technology will change. The models will get better. The economics will change with them.

There'll still be gold in the hills.

Go find it.
