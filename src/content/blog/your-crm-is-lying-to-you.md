I'm a recovering salesperson.

One of the things that comes with that particular condition is a healthy skepticism of the numbers on a marketing dashboard. I have the same skepticism of the numbers on a sales dashboard. Neither side gets a pass.

It isn't that I think the data is wrong. Sometimes it is perfectly accurate.

**It is that we mistake the data we have for the reality we need to understand.**

## A perfectly healthy pipeline

Let's imagine a technical company with a genuinely good product.

Marketing sees downloads, webinar registrations and event leads. Sales sees opportunities moving through a pipeline. Product sees adoption. The leadership team sees three sets of numbers that seem to suggest things are going reasonably well.

Then the quarter ends.

Revenue hasn't followed. Marketing wants to generate more leads. Sales wants better leads. Product wants more features. Everybody has a recommendation, and most of those recommendations involve spending money in their own department.

But what if the problem isn't any one of those departments?

What if the problem is what happens **between** them?

## BECOME a lead

Here is one of the first things I would do when trying to understand a company's go-to-market motion.

I would become a lead.

Go to the website. Try to work out what the product actually does. Find the documentation. Start the evaluation. See what happens when you express interest in buying. Where appropriate, test the process with the company's permission, without burning the team's time or polluting its reporting.

What message did you receive at each stage? Did it reflect what you were looking for? Did the next person know what the previous person knew?

More importantly, did the experience make it easier or harder for you to accomplish what you came there to do?

A CRM will happily tell you that a lead was created, qualified and passed to sales. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether the person on the other end of that process is any closer to solving their problem.

That's a fairly important distinction.

## The user is not necessarily the buyer

This is particularly interesting in commercial open source.

A developer can discover a product, test it, love it and deploy it without ever talking to the company behind it. That developer may eventually become the person who advocates for a commercial purchase.

But the developer's enthusiasm isn't the same thing as an enterprise buying decision.

The engineering manager might be worried about operational complexity. A security team might be concerned about access controls. The person approving the expenditure might want to know what risk is being removed or what other costs are being avoided.

These are not three unrelated audiences. They are people participating in the same decision.

If we spend all our time telling the developer how wonderfully clever the technology is, and none of our time helping that developer communicate its value to the rest of their organization, we shouldn't be surprised when adoption and revenue become disconnected.

The customer doesn't care which department owns that problem.

## Walk the floor

One of my favourite places to learn about positioning is a technical conference. Not the hotel meeting room where we spent the morning deciding what the message would be. The actual floor.

Say something to a practitioner. Did they understand it? Did they ask a question? Was it the question you expected?

Change a few words. Try again. Listen.

That is where you learn which parts of your story are useful and which parts sound like something five people approved in a conference room.

The badge scanner records a contact. The conversation gives you something potentially more valuable: an indication of what a real person cares about.

Both have their place. They are not interchangeable.

## Listen for both the gems and the turds

When I'm trying to diagnose commercial friction, I want to talk to the people who experience it.

Not just the executives who designed the process, and not just the top-performing rep. I want to hear from the people who have to make the system work every day. I want to know what they have worked around, what they have given up on, and what they wish someone would fix.

Then I want to compare those observations with the available data.

People have biases. Dashboards have blind spots. A useful diagnosis comes from making the two challenge each other.

There may be a positioning issue. It may be a qualification issue. It may be that the product generates plenty of interest but doesn't give the customer a compelling reason to buy. Or perhaps we are measuring a step in the journey that simply isn't that important.

I don't want to decide which one it is until we've looked.

## Fix the flow

Go-to-market is a system. Product, marketing, sales, customer success and the customer are all part of it.

When there's friction in that system, I think the most useful question isn't necessarily "How do we generate more?"

It's "Where is the value getting lost?"

Sometimes the answer is a new campaign. Sometimes it's different messaging, a better handoff, a product change, or a process we should stop doing altogether.

Before buying more demand, find out what is happening to the demand you already have.

That's the thinking behind my [GTM Friction Audit](/gtm#audit). Start with the business problem, investigate the evidence, then decide what needs fixing.

The CRM is one source of that evidence. It isn't the whole story.
